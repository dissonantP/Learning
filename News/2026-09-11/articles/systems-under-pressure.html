Conceptual art / current exhibitions

Systems under pressure

Six current or imminent shows use bodies, technology, ecology, collective labor, nightlife, and historical imagery to make systems of control physically legible.

September 11, 2026

This is not a “best exhibitions” list. The useful connection is methodological: each project makes an abstract system—modernity, racial surveillance, climate collapse, queer social space, image circulation—show up as something embodied, material, or participatory.

Lee Bul — utopia after the wreck

The Anatomy of Every Setting Sun, opened September 10 at Hauser & Wirth New York, extends Lee Bul’s long investigation of technological transcendence, modernist ambition, and systems of control. Her work is strongest when “the future” appears already archaeological: gleaming prosthetic and architectural forms read as both aspiration and ruin.

Chris Clark — the retail target becomes a social target

Clark paints on repurposed Target shopping bags, turning the familiar bullseye into a blunt symbol for how young Black men are watched and marked. The material choice began economically—cheap available support—but becomes the conceptual engine: consumer packaging doubles as a diagram of racial surveillance and threat.

Exhibition source

Re.tangle — social sculpture as repair infrastructure

Kiran Jandu and Anna Chapman build an 8-foot communal loom from reclaimed timber, emergency blankets, plastic waste, strings, and contact microphones. Visitors weave and repair the structure while their touch is amplified into sound. “Participation” here is not a polite add-on; the work literally changes only through collective maintenance.

Exhibition source

Gabrielle Turbide — a salt marsh that can suffocate

Dystrophe combines living photosynthetic microorganisms, chlorophyll printing, 3D prints, pumps, and electronics in a constructed salt marsh. The system models eutrophication: nutrient overload produces algal blooms, oxygen collapse, and ecological transition. The artwork is less a representation of environmental crisis than a small unstable ecology with its own feedback loops.

Exhibition source

Oscar yi Hou — nightlife as a temporary social technology

Opening September 11, Nightcrawling treats underground nightlife not merely as subject matter but as a charged state in which identity becomes collective, unstable, erotic, and exhausted. The paintings dissolve figure and environment into the same atmosphere. The useful idea is that queer space is made through repeated acts of gathering rather than existing as a neutral container.

Exhibition source

Arthur Jafa — image circulation as political material

The New Museum’s upcoming survey, opening September 24, gathers Jafa’s films, videos, photographs, paintings, and installations around a recurring problem: how to build a visual language adequate to Black life while working inside a culture that endlessly circulates images of Black culture and anti-Black violence. Jafa’s editing treats mass-media imagery almost like sampled sound—rhythm becomes argument.

Exhibition source

Also worth tracking: El Museo del Barrio opened Teddy Sandoval and the Butch Gardens School of Art on September 10. Sandoval’s fictional “school” grew from a Los Angeles gay bar into a conceptual framework for queer Chicane collaboration and an alternative to official art-world structures. Exhibition source.

The recurring move across these projects is to stop treating systems as invisible context. The target logo, the loom, the salt marsh, the nightclub, the archive, and the ruin all become interfaces through which power can be felt. That is the conceptual-art lineage worth following here: not “idea instead of object,” but object and situation redesigned so the idea becomes unavoidable.
