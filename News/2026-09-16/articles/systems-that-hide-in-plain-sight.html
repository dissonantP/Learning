Conceptual Art · Visual dossier · September 16, 2026

Systems that hide in plain sight

Four current projects use very different objects—museum vitrines, police footage, algorithmic organisms and intelligence archives—to make invisible systems legible. The common move is useful: instead of representing power symbolically, each artist borrows the system’s own formats and turns them against it.

Pablo Delano — The Museum of the Old Colony

Delano builds a fictional museum out of real colonial material concerning U.S. rule in Puerto Rico. The joke is structural: labels, archival photographs, official objects and anthropological display conventions manufacture authority before any individual claim is evaluated. The installation makes the museum itself the medium—and asks why imperial collecting practices look neutral once placed behind glass.

NBMAA exhibition and image source. On view in 2026.

Sable Elyse Smith — Clockwork

Smith’s exhibition treats policing, incarceration, entertainment and language as mutually reinforcing systems rather than separate subjects. For its September 24 New York opening, FLAG expands the video works into an immersive stage-set environment. Found police-chase/bodycam imagery is collided with humor, poetry and suspense: the point is not simply that violent images exist, but that television grammar teaches viewers how to consume them.

Her sculptural strategy is related: institutional furniture and familiar forms are transformed until punishment, play, belief and bureaucracy become difficult to separate.

FLAG Art Foundation — Clockwork, Sep 24 2026–Feb 13 2027.

Agnieszka Kurant — Recursion

Kurant’s recent work treats collective intelligence as something produced by systems rather than located in an individual mind. Termite mounds, language, crowds, bacteria and AI become comparable examples of emergent agency. That lets her connect a fashionable technical idea—distributed intelligence—to a political one: digital capitalism captures value created collectively while assigning ownership elsewhere.

Marian Goodman — Recursion; image ©/courtesy gallery, photo Alex Yudzon.

Taryn Simon — Father Country I Do Love You

Opening September 18 at the Guggenheim, Simon’s installation combines photographs, text, video and sculpture around the real life of a U.S. government agent moving through intelligence history. This is a characteristic Simon maneuver: bureaucracy’s obscure classifications, evidence practices and institutional stories become aesthetic material. Rather than inventing a conspiracy image, she reconstructs how state knowledge is actually formatted and authenticated.

Guggenheim — upcoming exhibition listing, Sep 18 2026–Mar 14 2027.

A connection worth keeping

Institutional critique is often described as art “about institutions.” These projects are sharper than that description suggests. They appropriate interfaces: museum taxonomy, reality-TV editing, computational emergence, evidentiary archives. Power becomes visible by reproducing the machinery through which it normally becomes mundane.
