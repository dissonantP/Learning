Art that behaves like evidence

A strand of conceptual art borrows the forms of investigation—records, diagrams, testimony, inventories and institutional display—then turns the evidentiary apparatus itself into the medium.

Hans Haacke — Shapolsky et al. (1971)

Haacke assembled 146 building photographs, maps and public real-estate records to trace a New York property network. The work looks deliberately bureaucratic: the aesthetic act is largely selection, linkage and display. The Guggenheim cancelled the planned exhibition six weeks before opening and dismissed its curator.

Whitney: work, image and history

Mark Lombardi — BCCI, ICIC & FAB (2000)

Lombardi converted years of research into elegant hand-drawn “narrative structures” connecting banks, governments, individuals and scandals. They sit oddly between drawing, investigative journalism and information design: the factual relationships are the picture.

Whitney: Lombardi overview · work

Silvia Kolbowski — An Inadequate History of Conceptual Art (1999)

Twenty-two artists recalled conceptual or performance works from the 1960s and ’70s without researching them or naming the works. Kolbowski films only their hands. The resulting oral archive is explicitly unreliable: memory becomes evidence while its instability remains visible.

Whitney: installation image · context

Fred Wilson — Guarded View (1991)

Four headless Black mannequins wear real security uniforms from New York museums. Wilson's broader practice treats curatorial arrangement as an artistic instrument: the museum's supposedly neutral classifications and staffing structures become material.

Whitney: Wilson and Guarded View

James Bridle — Drone Shadow (2012–)

Full-scale outlines of military drones are painted onto public streets. A remote, screen-mediated military object is translated into bodily scale. It is less a representation than a spatial disclosure: the hidden object's dimensions become locally undeniable.

MoMA: images and essay

Jon Kessler — The Palace at 4 A.M. (2005)

Kessler exposed the machinery of image production—cameras, screens, motors and surveillance-like feedback—inside an overwhelming installation about war, propaganda and mediated reality. Instead of hiding the apparatus, he makes apparatus the spectacle.

Why these belong together

Conceptual art's “dematerialization” can be misleading. These works are intensely material, but their material is administrative: property records, uniforms, testimony, network diagrams, optics, measurement. The move is not simply to make art about power. It is to appropriate the representational systems through which institutions know and operate on the world.

That also creates a useful connection to agent observability: both domains ask whether a trace merely records events or whether the structure of the trace determines what can be known. Haacke's dataset, Lombardi's graph and Kolbowski's deliberately faulty archive are three different answers.
