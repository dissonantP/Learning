Systems that watch back

Seven works in which surveillance, computation, labor and prediction stop being subject matter and become artistic material. The recurring move is appropriation: use the system’s own machinery against its normal purpose.

Trevor Paglen — It Began as a Military Experiment, 2017

Paglen re-presents faces from a US Defense Department dataset used to develop face recognition. Tiny facial landmarks make visible the conversion of a portrait into training data: the image is simultaneously a person and machine-readable infrastructure.

MoMA: work, images and context

Agnieszka Kurant
Recursion / Uncomputables

Agnieszka Kurant — Uncomputables, 2026

An electrochemical system grows copper crystals while a microcontroller modulates the process. Across Recursion, Kurant routes social, animal and predictive data through matter: algorithms do not illustrate emergence; they help produce objects that cannot be specified in advance.

Marian Goodman: extensive installation and work images

Minerva Cuevas
Mejor Vida Corp

Minerva Cuevas — Mejor Vida Corp, 1998–

A fictitious corporation distributed altered barcodes, student IDs and other interventions that could produce real economic benefits. Corporate identity becomes a parasitic interface into pricing and bureaucratic systems—the artwork behaves more like a service than an object.

Richard Mosse
Incoming

Richard Mosse — Incoming, 2014–17

Mosse uses military-grade thermal imaging designed for long-range border surveillance to film migration. The instrument erases skin color and conventional facial detail while exposing heat: a technology built to detect and classify bodies is redirected toward the human consequences of the border system using it.

Art21: images and “What the Camera Cannot See”

Rafael Lozano-Hemmer
Biometric participation

Rafael Lozano-Hemmer — biometric installations

Fingerprints, heartbeats, portraits and computerized tracking become collective public material. His long-running inversion is useful: surveillance technology still captures you, but the capture is made conspicuous, participatory and social rather than frictionless and hidden.

Art21: 36-work survey and videos

Mika Rottenberg
Mary’s Cherries

Mika Rottenberg — Mary’s Cherries, 2004

A grotesque fictional factory turns women’s fingernails into maraschino cherries. Bodies become machinery and bodily growth becomes commodity input. The absurdity is the mechanism: global production already contains transformations whose labor relations are nearly as strange, but ordinarily remain offstage.

Art21: gallery and films

Josh Kline
Automation, labor, climate

Josh Kline — automated workers and synthetic futures

Kline has 3D-scanned and printed workers in occupations threatened by automation, built a fictional UBI commercial, and staged a climate-refugee future. The commercial and technical formats are not neutral containers: they are precisely the languages through which the futures he critiques are sold.

Art21: artist profile and works

Connection worth keeping: these works resemble good systems research. Instead of commenting from outside, they instrument a mechanism—face datasets, thermal cameras, barcodes, biometrics, production lines—and expose what becomes visible when its normal abstraction boundary is broken.
